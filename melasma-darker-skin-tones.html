Melasma in darker skin tones: why it's harder to treat and what actually works

Melasma behaves the way it does because it was never only a pigment problem. Once you see what is actually damaged, everything else about it makes sense.

By Dr. Shankar Mundluru, MD MBA
Board-certified physician · Founder, Aahana Medical Aesthetics
September 4, 2026 · San Francisco

The short version

  • Melasma is not just overactive melanocytes. In nearly all lesions the barrier beneath the pigment cells is damaged.
  • That damage is why it recurs, and why single-agent treatment reliably disappoints.
  • Visible light drives melasma too, not only UV, which is why an untinted sunscreen is doing half a job.
  • Oral tranexamic acid is the most meaningful addition to treatment in years, with real prescribing caveats.
  • Aggressive treatment makes melasma worse more often than it makes it better.

Melasma is the one that comes back.

Patients describe the same arc. Something works. The patches across the cheeks or above the lip fade over a few months, they stop the routine or take a summer trip, and it returns to roughly where it started. After the second or third cycle they arrive assuming the problem is them, or that they simply haven't found the right product yet.

Neither is it. Melasma recurs because of what it actually is, and most explanations of it are describing the wrong layer of the skin.

What melasma is

Melasma appears as symmetric brown to gray-brown patches, most often across the cheeks, forehead, upper lip and jawline. It is strongly associated with sun exposure, with hormonal shifts including pregnancy and oral contraceptives, and with genetics. It overwhelmingly affects women, and it is far more common in people with darker skin.

That much is in every patient handout. The part that usually gets left out is what is happening underneath.

It is not a melanocyte problem

If melasma were only melanocytes making too much pigment, suppressing them would fix it. Decades of trying say otherwise. Biopsies point lower, to the dermis.1

95.8%of melasma lesions showed damage to the basement membrane, the boundary between epidermis and dermis
76% vs 42%pendulous melanocytes hanging into the dermis, lesion vs nearby skin
83% vs 29%solar elastosis, the signature of UV damage, lesion vs nearby skin

Through a damaged boundary, pigment cells and pigment drift down where they do not belong. The dermis is busy too: more mast cells releasing histamine and tryptase that drive pigment and break down collagen; more and larger vessels and higher VEGF, a sign of low-grade inflammation; and senescent fibroblasts sending pigment-promoting signals.1

The pigment is the symptom. The damaged boundary underneath it is the disease.

Why that explains how melasma behaves

Read that list again and every frustrating thing about this condition becomes predictable.

It recursThe damage is still there

The structural damage remains when the pigment fades. Lightening the surface does not repair a basement membrane.

Single agents disappointOne node of five

They address one part of a system with at least five, which is why the review's authors conclude melasma "is often recalcitrant and requires a multimodal approach."1

It worsens with agePhotoaging, not just sun

Genuinely photoaging-associated, not merely sun-triggered, so it tends to worsen across decades rather than resolving.

Not the goalCure

Melasma is a chronic relapsing condition.

The goalDurable control

I would rather say that at the first visit than let someone discover it after their third relapse.

Why it is harder in darker skin

Three things compound.

  1. More pigment to begin withThe same melanocyte overactivity produces a more visible result.
  2. More often dermalDermal pigment resists topicals, because most act on the epidermis.
  3. Riskier treatmentsIrritating peels and lasers can add post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation on top of the melasma.

The third turns a manageable condition into a two-year problem.

Conservative treatment is not timidity here. It is the strategy.

The light problem is bigger than UV

If you take one practical thing from this article, take this one.

Untinted sunscreenHalf the job

SPF only describes UVB. However high, it does very little about visible light, which is significantly underrecognized as a driver of hyperpigmentation in skin of color.2

Tinted, with iron oxidesBlocks visible light

More effective at preventing melasma relapse and reducing hyperpigmentation, and appears to help topical hydroquinone work. The guidance in skin of color: choose tinted with iron oxides.2

A genuinely cheap intervention that changes outcomes, and it is missed constantly. Visible light reaches you from:

ScreensIndoor lightingDaylight through a window

What actually works

Treatment is layered, and measured in months.

  1. FoundationPhotoprotection, tinted

    Not a supporting player. Every other agent is working against light exposure.

  2. CoreTopical therapy

    Hydroquinone, usually as a triple combination with a retinoid and a short course of low-potency corticosteroid, has the longest record. Azelaic acid, cysteamine, niacinamide and topical tranexamic acid are reasonable additions or alternatives. Combinations beat single agents, for the structural reasons above.

  3. The biggest addition in yearsOral tranexamic acid

    A network meta-analysis of six randomized trials (599 patients) found 750 mg a day, as 250 mg three times daily for twelve weeks, the best regimen, with 250 mg twice daily acceptable when adherence is a concern.3 Serious clotting events were rare at these doses, far below those used in coagulation medicine; stomach upset and lighter periods are common.3

Before tranexamic acid

It is an antifibrinolytic and a prescription medication that needs a real history first. Personal or family history of clotting, current hormonal contraception, smoking, immobility, recent surgery and pregnancy all belong in that conversation. Anyone offering it without that history is skipping the part that matters.

Where peels and lasers fit

Cautiously, and usually late. Melasma is notorious for being made worse by aggressive procedures.

  • Superficial peelsAs an adjunct, once topical therapy has stabilized things.
  • Lasers, at low fluenceConservative settings. In deeper skin the 1064 nm Nd:YAG is the usual choice, for reasons in a separate article.
The common failure

A device used at settings meant for a different problem, the skin inflames, and the patient leaves with melasma plus post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. With the basement membrane already damaged in nearly every lesion, adding thermal injury is not obviously wise. Procedures follow topical control; they do not replace it.

What to expect

A realistic plan runs on months, layers several agents, treats sun protection as treatment rather than advice, and assumes maintenance rather than a finish line. Relapse after a summer, a pregnancy or a lapse in photoprotection is the expected behavior of the condition, not evidence that the plan failed.

Melasma responds well to patience and consistency. It punishes impatience more reliably than almost anything else we treat.

Common questions

Why is melasma so hard to treat?

Because it is not only a pigment problem. Biopsy studies find basement membrane damage in about 96 percent of melasma lesions, along with pendulous melanocytes reaching into the dermis, increased mast cells, increased vascularity and solar elastosis. Lightening the surface does not repair any of that, which is why melasma recurs and why single-agent treatment usually disappoints.

Can melasma be cured?

Melasma is best understood as a chronic relapsing condition managed rather than cured. Durable control is realistic. Permanent clearance, particularly without ongoing photoprotection and maintenance therapy, generally is not. Relapse after sun exposure, pregnancy or a hormonal change is expected behavior rather than treatment failure.

Does sunscreen actually help melasma?

Yes, and it is foundational rather than supplementary. But visible light drives pigmentation in skin of color as well as ultraviolet, and untinted sunscreen offers little protection against visible light regardless of SPF. Tinted sunscreens containing iron oxides are more effective than untinted ones at preventing melasma relapse and may improve the performance of topical hydroquinone.

Does tranexamic acid work for melasma?

Oral tranexamic acid has good evidence in melasma. A network meta-analysis of six randomized trials covering 599 patients found 750 mg daily, as 250 mg three times daily for twelve weeks, to be the optimal dose, with 250 mg twice daily acceptable for adherence. Serious clotting complications were rare at these doses, but it is an antifibrinolytic prescription medication and requires a proper history covering clotting risk, hormonal contraception, smoking and pregnancy.

Is laser treatment safe for melasma in dark skin?

It can be, with conservative settings and after topical therapy has stabilized the condition. The 1064 nm Nd:YAG at low fluence is the usual choice in deeper skin. Aggressive laser treatment is a well-recognized way to make melasma worse, because thermal injury can add post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation on top of the melasma. Procedures should follow topical control rather than replace it.

Why is melasma more common in darker skin?

Greater baseline pigment means the same melanocyte overactivity produces a more visible result. Melasma in darker skin also more often has a dermal component, which resists topical treatment because most topicals act on the epidermis. And treatments carry more risk, since peels and lasers that irritate can trigger post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation layered on top of the original condition.
