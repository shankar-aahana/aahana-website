Daybreak · Skin of color

Why most laser treatments weren't designed for your skin

The problem was never melanin. It was that the devices, the settings, and the studies behind them were built around a narrower range of skin than the one that actually walks into a clinic.

By Dr. Shankar Mundluru, MD MBA
Board-certified in Internal Medicine · Founder, Aahana Medical Aesthetics
September 4, 2026 · San Francisco

The short version

  • Laser safety in darker skin is a property of the device and its settings, not of the patient.
  • Melanin in the epidermis absorbs laser energy meant for a deeper target, so the wavelength has to be chosen to get past it.
  • Longer wavelengths such as 1064 nm Nd:YAG are absorbed far less by melanin than the shorter wavelengths used in most pigment and hair devices.
  • Broadband IPL cannot avoid this problem, which is why it causes a disproportionate share of burns and pigment changes in skin of color.
  • "FDA cleared" does not mean "studied in skin like yours." Most dermatologic trials still don't report skin type at all.

A version of this conversation happens in every consultation room in the country. Someone comes in having read about a treatment, or having watched a friend get a beautiful result from it, and is told, sometimes gently and sometimes not, that they aren't a candidate. Their skin is too dark. It's too risky. Come back with a different question.

I want to be precise about what is actually being said in that moment, because it is almost never what the patient hears. The patient hears: there is something about my skin that is a problem. What is usually true is narrower and much more fixable: the device in that room, at the settings that practice runs, is not appropriate for your skin.

Those are not the same sentence. One is a statement about a person. The other is a statement about equipment and training. The first has been repeated so often that it now functions as received wisdom, and it has kept a very large number of people out of care they could have had safely.

So let me take the physics apart, because once you see how a laser actually makes its decisions, the whole thing stops being mysterious, and you get a much better set of questions to ask whoever is holding the handpiece.

What the Fitzpatrick scale was actually built for

Start with the number everyone gets assigned. If you've had a consultation, someone has probably called you a Fitzpatrick III, or IV, or V.

That scale was developed by Thomas Fitzpatrick at Harvard in 1975, and it was not designed to describe skin color. It was designed to solve a dosing problem. Fitzpatrick was treating psoriasis with PUVA, oral psoralen followed by ultraviolet A, and he needed to predict how much UVA a given patient could tolerate before burning. Hair and eye color turned out to be unreliable predictors. So he built a four-category system based on how skin responded to sun: who burns, who tans, who does both.2

Four categories. All of them describing lighter skin. Types V and VI were appended later, which is why they have always felt like an afterthought: two boxes asked to hold an enormous range of human pigmentation that the original instrument was never built to resolve.

This matters practically, not just historically. A scale built to predict sunburn is being used as a proxy for melanin content, for ancestry, and for laser risk. None of those are things it measures. Misclassification is well documented and is worst in exactly the skin types where the consequences of getting it wrong are highest. Objective colorimetry outperforms it. And yet the Fitzpatrick number is still, in most clinics, the entire risk assessment.

A scale invented to predict sunburn is now doing the work of a safety protocol.

How a laser decides what to damage

Every aesthetic laser works on a principle called selective photothermolysis, described by Anderson and Parrish in 1983.1 The idea is elegant: choose a wavelength that your intended target absorbs more strongly than the surrounding tissue does, deliver it in a pulse short enough that the heat stays in the target rather than spreading, and you can destroy something very small and very specific while leaving its neighbors intact.

The absorbing structure is called the chromophore. In aesthetic medicine there are essentially three that matter: hemoglobin, water, and melanin. A vascular laser is aiming at hemoglobin. An ablative resurfacing laser is aiming at water. A hair removal or pigment laser is aiming at melanin, either in the hair shaft or in a sunspot.

And here is the entire problem, in one sentence: when your target is melanin, and your epidermis is also full of melanin, the light has to pass through a layer made of the exact thing it was designed to be absorbed by.

Epidermal melanin becomes a competing chromophore. It intercepts energy intended for something deeper and converts it to heat in the wrong place, at the surface, where it produces blistering, crusting and pigment injury rather than the intended effect. This is the mechanism behind essentially every bad laser outcome in darker skin. The standard clinical guidance follows directly from it: in darker skin types, use longer wavelengths, lower fluences, and longer pulse durations to reduce thermal injury to the epidermis.3

Why wavelength is the single most important choice

Melanin absorbs light across a broad range, but not evenly. Its absorption falls off steadily as wavelength increases. Shorter wavelengths, the greens and yellows around 500 to 600 nm that are excellent at picking up superficial sunspots on fair skin, are absorbed intensely by epidermal melanin. Push out toward the near infrared and that absorption drops by roughly an order of magnitude.

This is why the 1064 nm Nd:YAG has become the backbone of laser treatment in skin of color. At that wavelength the light penetrates more deeply and is far less likely to interact with the melanosomes sitting in the epidermis on its way down. More of the energy reaches the target. The ratio between the temperature at the target and the temperature at the surface improves dramatically, and that ratio is precisely what determines whether a treatment works or burns.3

The same logic explains why ablative erbium:YAG at 2940 nm behaves differently again. At that wavelength the dominant chromophore is water, not melanin, and water is distributed through the tissue regardless of pigmentation. The pigment-competition problem largely disappears. What does not disappear is the healing response, and I want to be honest about that, because it is where a lot of marketing gets sloppy. A wavelength that ignores melanin on the way in does not make the skin ignore the injury afterward.

Where IPL goes wrong

Intense pulsed light is not a laser. It's a broadband flashlamp. It emits a wide spectrum at once, filtered rather than tuned. On fair skin this versatility is a genuine strength; one device addresses redness, brown spots and texture.

On darker skin it is a structural problem, not a settings problem. A meaningful fraction of that broadband output sits in exactly the short wavelengths that epidermal melanin absorbs most strongly. You cannot filter your way out of it and still have a device that does anything. The energy is deposited at the surface before it ever reaches the intended target.

IPL is, in my view, responsible for a disproportionate share of the burns, blistering and long-standing pigment changes that patients with medium and deep skin tones arrive carrying. Not because it is a bad technology, but because it is a technology that was matched to the wrong patient.

Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is not a minor side effect

The most common adverse outcome in this population is post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, or PIH. It deserves more than a line on a consent form.

Melanocytes in richly pigmented skin are not more numerous; they are more reactive. Inflammation of nearly any kind, whether a burn, acne, a scratch or an overaggressive laser pass, can trigger them to overproduce pigment during healing. The result is a darkened patch that appears after the treatment, sometimes weeks after, exactly where the energy went.

When that pigment stays in the epidermis, it typically fades over six to twelve months with rigorous sun protection and topical therapy. When inflammation is severe enough to breach the dermal-epidermal junction, pigment drops into the dermis, where it can persist for years and may never fully resolve.

Sit with the cruelty of that for a moment. A patient comes in for uneven pigmentation. The treatment produces more of it, in a form that is harder to treat than what she started with, and she is told to wait a year. This is the outcome the entire conversation is trying to prevent, and it is overwhelmingly preventable through device selection and conservative parameters.

The risk in darker skin is not that the treatment won't work. It's that the wrong treatment produces the exact problem the patient came in to solve.

What "FDA cleared" does and does not tell you

Patients reasonably assume that a cleared device has been demonstrated safe in skin like theirs. Often it has not.

An analysis of dermatology trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov covering the fifteen most common dermatologic conditions found 447 completed trials with published results. Only 278 of them, or 62.2 percent, reported participants' race, ethnicity, or Fitzpatrick skin type at all. Among those that did, 69.1 percent of participants were white.4 Systematic reviews looking specifically at laser and light-based trials have reached the same conclusion: types V and VI are consistently underrepresented, including for conditions that disproportionately affect them.5

So a clearance can be entirely legitimate and still rest on an evidence base that barely looked at your skin. That is not a scandal, exactly. It is a gap. But it means the burden of judgment shifts onto the person operating the device, and you are entitled to know how they exercise it.

How we chose the devices at Aahana

We're opening in Dogpatch, in a city where a practice that can only treat a narrow range of skin is a practice that cannot serve its own neighborhood. San Francisco does not have a majority skin tone. Building a device stack around one would have been both a clinical failure and an arithmetic one.

So the constraint came first, before the device list: every platform in the clinic has to be appropriate across the full range of skin we expect to see. That ruled things out. It ruled out broadband IPL as a primary pigment modality entirely.

Our primary laser platform is the Aerolase Neo Elite, a 1064 nm Nd:YAG with a 650-microsecond pulse, FDA cleared across Fitzpatrick types I through VI. The wavelength does the first half of the work: at 1064 nm the light penetrates more deeply and is far less likely to interact with melanosomes in the epidermis on the way down. The pulse duration does the second half. At 650 microseconds the energy is delivered and gone before much heat can accumulate at the surface of the skin, which means two things at once. The treatment is more comfortable, and there is less thermal energy sitting in the epidermis to provoke the melanocyte response that produces pigment problems in the first place.

Alongside it we run an erbium:YAG at 2940 nm for ablative work, where the chromophore is water rather than melanin, and monopolar radiofrequency for tissue tightening. Radiofrequency is not light at all, so pigment plays no part in its mechanism and skin tone does not constrain it.

Narrow is the point. I would rather run a small number of platforms that are genuinely appropriate for everyone who walks in than a large menu with an asterisk next to half of it. You can see the full stack on our services page.

Eight questions worth asking before anyone treats you

You do not need to become a laser physicist. You need to be able to tell, in about four minutes, whether the person in front of you has thought about this. These are the questions that reveal it.

  1. What wavelength is this device, in nanometers?A specific number should come back immediately. "It's our pigment laser" is not an answer.
  2. Is this a laser or IPL?Ask directly. The two are often used interchangeably in marketing and they are not interchangeable on your skin.
  3. How many patients with my skin tone have you treated on this specific device?Experience with the wavelength matters more than years in practice generally.
  4. What settings will you use, and are they different from what you'd use on lighter skin?If the answer is that nothing changes, that is the answer you were looking for.
  5. Will you do a test spot first, and how long will you wait before assessing it?Pigment changes are delayed. A test spot read at ten minutes tells you very little.
  6. What is your protocol if I develop hyperpigmentation?A practice that treats skin of color routinely will have a plan already. A practice that doesn't will improvise.
  7. Do you pre-treat before laser, and with what?Pre- and post-treatment with agents such as hydroquinone reduces PIH risk after resurfacing.3
  8. Who is operating the device, and who is supervising?You are entitled to know both, and to know what happens if something goes wrong mid-treatment.

A good provider will welcome all eight. If any of them produce defensiveness, you have learned something useful for the price of asking.

The part I actually care about

There is a version of inclusive care that is a sentence on a website. There is another version that changes what you buy, who you hire, what you decline to offer, and how long your consultations run. Only one of those is worth anything to a patient sitting on the table.

The physics here has been settled for forty years. Anderson and Parrish published in 1983. The wavelength behavior of melanin is not in dispute. What has lagged is not the science. It's the willingness to build a practice around it, when the shortcut of buying one versatile device and treating a narrower slice of the population has always been cheaper.

If you've been told your skin is too dark for a treatment you wanted, it is worth finding out whether that was a statement about your skin or about the room you were standing in. Frequently it was the room.

Common questions

Is laser treatment safe for dark skin?

Yes, when the wavelength and settings are chosen for your skin. Safety in darker skin is a property of the device and its parameters, not of the patient. Longer wavelengths such as 1064 nm Nd:YAG are absorbed far less by epidermal melanin than the shorter wavelengths used in most pigment and hair devices, which is why they are the standard choice for Fitzpatrick types IV through VI.

Which laser is safest for Fitzpatrick types V and VI?

The 1064 nm Nd:YAG is the most widely used wavelength for types V and VI, because melanin absorbs light progressively less as wavelength increases and less energy is intercepted by the epidermis on the way to the target. Ablative erbium:YAG at 2940 nm targets water rather than melanin, which removes the pigment-competition problem, though not the risk of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation from the healing response itself.

Why is IPL not recommended for darker skin tones?

IPL is broadband rather than a single tuned wavelength, so a significant portion of its output falls in the shorter wavelengths that epidermal melanin absorbs strongly. In darker skin there is no way to deliver that spectrum to a deeper target without depositing substantial energy in the epidermis first, which is why IPL is a common cause of burns, blistering and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation in skin of color.

Can laser treatment cause hyperpigmentation?

Yes. Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is the most common adverse effect of laser treatment in skin of color. Any injury or inflammation can stimulate melanocytes to overproduce pigment during healing, leaving darkened patches. The risk rises with epidermal heating, which is why device selection and conservative settings matter more in darker skin.

How long does post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation last?

Epidermal PIH typically fades over six to twelve months with strict sun protection and topical treatment. When pigment reaches the dermis it can persist for years and may be only partially reversible, which is why prevention through correct device selection matters far more than treating it after the fact.

Should I have a test spot before laser treatment?

Yes. A test spot in a discreet area, assessed after a delay long enough for delayed pigment changes to appear, is standard practice in darker skin. A provider who declines to test before a full-face treatment is skipping the single most informative safety step available.
