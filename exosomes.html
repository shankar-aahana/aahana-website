Topical exosomes

Applied to the skin after microneedling. An emerging area with real published work behind it and real gaps in it, and no FDA approval. Here is all of that plainly.

Performed by Dr. Shankar Mundluru, MD MBA, a board-certified physician

Exosomes are extracellular vesicles, small packets that cells release and that carry proteins and genetic material between cells. They are the subject of a large amount of laboratory research and a large amount of marketing, and those two bodies of work are not the same size.

01

Regulatory status, before anything else

No exosome product has FDA approval for any aesthetic use, whether it is injected or applied to the skin. The FDA does not treat topical application as a separate category, and it has issued warning letters and a public safety notification about exosome products. Anyone who tells you otherwise is either mistaken or selling something.

That is the first thing to know, and it is the reason this page exists as an explanation rather than a pitch. If you are not comfortable with an unapproved product, that is a reasonable position and it will not be argued with.

What is used here, and how

Only topically, and only after microneedling. Nothing is injected. The preparation is applied to skin immediately after treatment, while the channels created by microneedling are still open, and it is offered as an addition to that treatment rather than as a treatment of its own.

What the published work reports

A 2026 systematic review in the Journal of Cosmetic Dermatology gathered the clinical studies of microneedling with exosomes. It found eight studies covering 171 patients, screened from 256 references.

Across those studies, investigators reported increases in hair density in androgenetic alopecia, wrinkle reduction in the range of 12 to 14 percent, and improvement in pigmentation measures, with objective dark spot coverage falling from 40 percent to 27 percent in the studies that measured it. No patient in any of the studies discontinued treatment because of an adverse effect. Redness, swelling and small areas of bruising occurred and resolved within days to a week.

What that evidence does not establish

The review's authors are direct about the limitations, and they matter more here than usual.

Small studiesEight studies, 171 patients in total. Case series and split-face pilots rather than large randomized trials
Short follow-upNo study followed patients beyond six months
Different productsExosome sources differed between studies, so results do not necessarily transfer from one preparation to another
No standardThere is no agreed definition of what a given product contains, and no requirement that it be verified

The authors conclude that adequately powered randomized controlled trials are needed before firm conclusions about safety and efficacy can be drawn. That is where this stands, and it should be weighed against the confidence with which exosomes are usually marketed.

How this is offered

As an optional addition to microneedling, at additional cost, after a conversation in which the above is said out loud rather than buried in a consent form. It is not recommended by default, it is not part of any standard protocol here, and declining it changes nothing about the treatment you came for.

Common questions

Is this FDA approved?

No. No exosome product has FDA approval for any aesthetic use, by any route. The FDA has issued warning letters and a public safety notification regarding exosome products, and it does not treat topical application as exempt from that. This is stated here rather than left for you to discover, because it should be part of the decision rather than a footnote to it.

Why offer it at all, then?

Because there is published clinical work behind it, because the reported safety profile in those studies was good, and because patients ask for it and will otherwise seek it somewhere that does not tell them what this page tells them. It is offered topically, as an addition, with the evidence and the regulatory status said plainly. If that combination does not sit right with you, the answer is to decline it, and that is a completely reasonable answer.

Do you inject exosomes?

No. Only topical application after microneedling. Injected exosome products are where the reported adverse events and the regulatory attention have been concentrated, and there is no version of that offered here.

How strong is the evidence, honestly?

Early. Eight studies covering 171 patients in total, mostly case series and split-face pilots rather than large randomized trials, with no follow-up beyond six months and different exosome sources between studies. The results reported were positive and the authors of the review still concluded that adequately powered randomized trials are needed before firm conclusions can be drawn. That is a fair summary and it is deliberately not dressed up.

Is it better than PRP?

Nobody knows, because the studies that would answer that have not been done. PRP has a considerably larger evidence base and uses your own blood rather than a manufactured product, which are two real differences. What is appropriate for you depends on what is being treated, and that is a consultation question rather than a ranking.

Who should not have this?

Anyone who is not comfortable with an unapproved product, which is a sufficient reason on its own. Beyond that, the exclusions are those for microneedling itself: active infection or inflammatory skin disease in the treatment area, certain wound-healing disorders, and pregnancy. Anyone with a history of significant allergic reaction should raise it before anything is applied.

How to prepare

Three days before

  • Stop over-the-counter anti-inflammatories. Ibuprofen, naproxen, fish oil, vitamin E and flaxseed oil. These reduce platelet activity and directly undermine the treatment. This is the most important instruction on this page.
  • Stop alcohol. It affects platelet function and increases bruising.
  • Stop high-dose vitamin C above 1,000 mg a day.
  • Do not stop prescribed blood thinners or anticoagulants such as aspirin taken for cardiac reasons, apixaban, rivaroxaban, warfarin or clopidogrel. Do not stop any of these without speaking to the doctor who prescribed them. Contact us instead and we will plan around them.

Tell us before your appointment

  • Any clotting disorder or platelet condition.
  • Any history of blood cancer, or any active cancer. PRP is not performed in either case.
  • Any active infection, rash or open wound in the treatment area.
  • Your current medications, particularly corticosteroids and immunosuppressants, which affect platelet function and healing.
  • For scalp treatment, any scalp condition such as psoriasis, seborrheic dermatitis or a fungal infection. These are treated first.
  • If you are pregnant or breastfeeding.
  • Any known allergies.

The day itself

  • Drink water and eat something. Hydration measurably affects the quality of the plasma drawn.
  • Arrive with the treatment area clean and no products on it.
  • For scalp treatment, wash your hair that morning with a gentle shampoo and use no styling product.
  • Wear something comfortable. A button-front top is easier for facial treatment.

The first 24 hours

  • No anti-inflammatories for three days. No ibuprofen, no naproxen. The inflammatory response after treatment is part of how PRP works, and suppressing it reduces the result. If you need something for discomfort, ask us what to take instead.
  • No ice directly on the skin. It constricts vessels and dampens the same response. A gentle cool compress is fine if swelling is marked.
  • Swelling, redness and small bumps at the injection sites are normal, particularly under the eye and on the scalp.
  • Do not touch, rub or massage the injection sites.
  • No vigorous exercise for 24 hours.
  • Avoid heat: saunas, steam rooms and very hot showers.
  • No alcohol for 24 hours.
  • Gentle skincare is fine on the face from the evening: cleanser and a fragrance-free moisturizer. SPF 50 the next morning.

When to contact us straight away

  • Any change in vision after facial or under-eye treatment. Seek emergency care immediately.
  • Swelling that is worsening after 48 hours rather than settling.
  • Signs of infection at any injection site: spreading redness, warmth, pus or fever.
  • Numbness or tingling that lasts beyond 24 hours.
  • On the scalp, significant pain or swelling, or any sign of infection.
