Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation: what it is, why it happens, and how we treat it

The dark mark left behind after acne clears is not a scar, and it is not permanent. It is also the most common reason patients with deeper skin come to see me.

By Dr. Shankar Mundluru, MD MBA
Board-certified physician · Founder, Aahana Medical Aesthetics
September 4, 2026 · San Francisco

The short version

  • PIH is pigment left behind after inflammation. It is not a scar, and the surface of the skin is intact.
  • Among people with darker skin who have acne, up to 65 percent develop it.
  • Whether the pigment sits in the epidermis or the dermis decides whether it takes months or years.
  • Nothing works reliably until the inflammation causing it is under control.
  • Sun exposure re-darkens it faster than any treatment lightens it.

The spot healed weeks ago. What is left is a flat brown mark exactly where it was, and it has not moved since. Makeup covers it. Nothing else seems to.

Patients often arrive convinced this is scarring, and braced for the answer that it is permanent. It usually isn't. What they have is post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, and the distinction matters, because a scar is a change in the structure of the skin and this is not. Run a finger across it and the surface is smooth. Nothing was lost. Something was only left behind.

What post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation actually is

Inflammation is not a quiet process.

  1. Injury or irritationSkin releases inflammatory cytokines, prostaglandins and reactive oxygen species.1
  2. Melanocytes respondThe signals reach the pigment cells at the base of the epidermis, which make more melanin.
  3. Handed upwardMelanin passes to keratinocytes and rises with them as skin turns over.
  4. The markA flat patch of extra pigment in the exact footprint of the inflammation.

Acne is by far the most common trigger, but all of these produce the same response:1

AcneEczemaAn insect biteA scratchA burnA chemical peelAn overaggressive laser pass

The skin is not malfunctioning. It is doing exactly what it evolved to do.

The mark is the record of an injury, not a defect.

Why it lands harder on darker skin

Melanocytes in richly pigmented skin are not more numerous. They are more responsive: greater baseline melanin and more reactive melanocytes mean the same signal produces a larger pigment response.1

Up to 65%Incidence of PIH among people with darker skin tones who have acne1
A week vs a yearHow patients describe it: the breakout lasted a week, the mark has lasted a year

Particularly common in people of African, Asian and Latin American descent, where the pigment left behind is often more distressing than the acne that caused it.

Why depth decides the timeline

This decides what I can honestly promise.1

Clears in 6 to 12 months
faster with treatment; the epidermis renews and the pigment leaves with it
Dermal · blue-gray
Improves slowly, may be permanent
inflammation breached the junction; immune cells (melanophages) hold the pigment, and the dermis does not turn over

A Wood lamp helps tell them apart in the room.1 One is a waiting problem, the other a management problem. A provider who tells you which, before selling a package, is doing the part that matters.

Brown tends to leave. Blue-gray tends to stay. Knowing which you have is the whole conversation.

The first rule is to treat the inflammation, not the mark

This is where most PIH treatment goes wrong, in an understandable way. The pigment is what you can see, so the pigment gets treated.

Treating the mark firstBailing without patching

If acne or eczema is still active, every new lesion makes new pigment faster than the old clears.

The sequenceControl inflammation first

Not negotiable.1 Then treat what it left behind.

Which is why some of the best agents for PIH treat both at once. When one agent closes the tap and drains the sink, use it:1

Retinoids: acne and pigmentAzelaic acid: acne and pigment

Sunscreen is the treatment, not the accessory

Every topical is working against the sun, and the sun is patient.

Ultraviolet exposure intensifies existing pigment and prolongs the time to resolution.1 I raise this early, not as a footnote: daily photoprotection does more of the work than most people believe, and a plan without it underperforms in a way that looks like the treatment failing. Recurrence is common, and inadequate sun protection is the usual reason.1

What actually works on the pigment

Stepwise, combined, and measured in months.1

  1. MainstayHydroquinone

    Blocks tyrosinase, the enzyme melanocytes need to make melanin, with the longest record. A common triple combination: hydroquinone 4%, tretinoin 0.05% and a low-potency corticosteroid, the steroid deliberately limited to about eight weeks.1

  2. Long-termRetinoids and azelaic acid

    Tretinoin, adapalene and tazarotene speed turnover and treat acne at the same time.1 Azelaic acid does double duty and is often better tolerated.

  3. NewerTopical cysteamine

    Among the more potent options now available for pigmentation.1

  4. Alternative and maintenanceTranexamic acid

    Meaningful evidence in melasma and PIH; useful if hydroquinone is not tolerated, or for maintenance.2

None is dramatic alone. Combined, used consistently, and given months, they work.

Where peels and lasers fit

Both can help. Both can cause the exact problem you are treating.

Chemical peelsGlycolic, salicylic, TCA

Remove epidermal cells carrying the excess pigment. In darker skin, a cautious hand: a peel that irritates too much makes new inflammation, and new pigment.1

LasersQ-switched, picosecond

Same double edge. Conservative parameters in deeper skin, and an operator who understands why.1 More in a separate piece.

The clinical logic, in one line

Someone with a history of PIH has told you, in advance, exactly how her skin will answer an aggressive treatment.

This condition rewards patience and punishes aggression; most of the damage I see was done by treatments that promised to be faster.

Common questions

What is post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation?

Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation is a flat dark patch left behind after skin inflammation heals. Inflammatory cytokines, prostaglandins and reactive oxygen species stimulate melanocytes to overproduce melanin, which is deposited in the epidermis and sometimes the dermis, in the exact footprint of the original injury. Acne is the most common cause.

Is PIH a scar?

No. A scar is a change in the structure of the skin, with tissue lost or replaced. PIH is only a change in color, and the surface remains smooth. That is why PIH can fade and true scarring cannot.

How long does post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation take to fade?

Epidermal PIH, which appears brown, typically resolves or significantly improves within six to twelve months, and faster with treatment. Dermal PIH, which appears blue-gray, improves slowly and may be permanent. A Wood lamp examination can distinguish the two.

Does PIH go away on its own?

Epidermal PIH often does, given enough time and strict sun protection, though treatment shortens the timeline considerably. Dermal PIH is far less likely to clear without intervention. In either case, pigment will keep being replaced if the underlying inflammation, most often acne, is still active.

What is the best treatment for PIH in dark skin?

There is no single best agent. Effective treatment is stepwise and combines daily sun protection, control of the underlying inflammatory condition, and topical therapy. Hydroquinone remains the mainstay, often as a triple combination with tretinoin and a short course of a low-potency steroid. Retinoids and azelaic acid treat acne and pigment at once. Cysteamine and tranexamic acid are useful additions, particularly for patients who cannot tolerate hydroquinone.

Can laser treatment fix PIH?

Sometimes, with caution. Q-switched and picosecond lasers are used for pigment, but in darker skin they require conservative settings and an experienced operator, because laser injury is itself a cause of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. A patient who has already developed PIH has demonstrated that her skin responds to injury by producing pigment, which argues for a conservative approach.
