Visia skin analysis

Eight features of your skin photographed under standardized light, including damage sitting below the surface that has not emerged yet. It is where every plan here starts.

Almost every decision people make about their skin is made in front of a mirror, in whatever light they happen to be standing in, filtered through a lifetime of opinions about their own face. It is a poor instrument for a decision that costs money and takes months to judge.

Visia is an imaging system built by Canfield Scientific. You rest your chin and forehead in a fixed position inside an enclosed booth and it takes a short series of photographs under controlled lighting, including ultraviolet. Same light, same angle, same camera, every visit. That alone makes two appointments comparable, which a mirror and a memory never are.

Included in every consultation

Imaging happens before any treatment is discussed, for everyone, at no additional charge. Nothing gets recommended here until there is something on the screen to recommend it from.

What it photographs

Eight features, three of which are not visible to the eye at all.1

SpotsSurface pigmentation, including marks you can already see
Brown spotsMelanin-based pigment, the channel that matters for melasma and post-inflammatory marks
Red areasVascular concentration and inflammation, frequently invisible against brown and olive skin
UV spotsPigment damage absorbed below the surface that has not emerged yet
PorphyrinsBacterial excretions in the follicle, which fluoresce under the right light
PoresSize distribution and congestion across the face
TextureSurface irregularity, the thing that changes how light sits on skin
WrinklesFine lines and deeper creases, mapped and scored rather than estimated

The image that changes the conversation

UV spots are subsurface damage from ultraviolet light absorbed by melanin.1 The pigment is already there. It has not reached the surface.

Every other measurement on the report describes your skin now. This one describes your skin later. Someone in her early thirties with a clean surface and heavy UV spotting is looking at the next fifteen years of her face at the exact moment when sun protection can still change where it ends up. Telling someone that sun exposure accumulates is an abstraction. Showing her the accumulation, on her own face, is not.

Pulling red apart from brown

The system uses a process called RBX, which separates the red and brown color signatures in skin so each can be looked at on its own.1 That is more useful than it sounds, and it is disproportionately useful in richly pigmented skin.

Discoloration across the cheeks could be melasma, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, background vascular flushing, or two of those at once. Against a deeper skin tone these can look nearly identical in the room. They are treated differently, and treating one as though it were another is how people end up worse rather than better. Separating the channels answers the question visually in a few seconds. It is not a diagnosis on its own, but it narrows the field considerably before anyone touches your skin.

What it changes about your plan

Three things, in practice.

It shows what is actually present rather than what is most visible. The loudest thing on a face is rarely the thing with the most to gain from treatment, and the quiet thing is often the one that will matter in ten years.

It establishes a baseline, which is the only way the next visit means anything. Without one, judging whether a treatment worked means comparing today against a memory, and memory is both unreliable and heavily biased toward believing the money was well spent. A measured baseline makes it possible to be wrong, which is the only condition under which being right counts for something.

And it changes the consultation itself. You see your own skin the way a clinician sees it, which turns a list of recommendations into a conversation about evidence you are both looking at.

What it does not do

It measures. It does not diagnose. Visia quantifies features; it does not identify skin cancer, and it is not a substitute for examination by a clinician.

Its numbers also deserve a little skepticism. In a reproducibility study, repeated captures showed standard deviations of roughly 3 percent for absolute wrinkle scores and roughly 9 percent for percentiles, which makes absolute scores the better thing to track.2 The same study found the calculated skin age correlated strongly with chronological age across the group, while individual deviations ran from seven years younger to nine years older.2 And that study involved 19 participants and did not examine performance across different skin types or ethnicities.2 That gap is a familiar one. It is a reason to read the pictures carefully rather than lean on a number, particularly in deeper skin.

Where it sits among the other measurements

Imaging describes what your skin has done so far. Microbiome sequencing describes what is living on it right now and what that is producing. Lab work describes what is driving it from the inside. Imaging is the one everybody gets; the other two are ordered only where they would change something.

Common questions

Does Visia diagnose anything?

No. It measures features and reports numbers. It does not identify skin cancer and it is not a substitute for examination by a clinician. Anything that looks concerning on an image gets examined properly in the room, and anything concerning on your skin should be examined whether or not it showed up in a photograph.

Does it work on darker skin?

The images do, and they are arguably more useful in deeper skin, because separating the red channel from the brown answers a question the eye cannot. The derived numbers deserve more caution. The published reproducibility study involved 19 participants and did not examine performance across different skin types or ethnicities, so in deeper skin the pictures carry more weight here than the percentiles do. More on why that gap keeps appearing is in the piece on skin of color and the evidence base.

How should I prepare for imaging?

Come with a clean face: no makeup, and no sunscreen applied that morning. Both sit on the surface and interfere with the ultraviolet capture, which is the image with the most to say. Nothing else is required. There is no fasting, no numbing and no downtime.

What is TruSkin Age and should I take it seriously?

It is a calculated skin age derived from your scores against a normative database. It correlated strongly with chronological age across a study group, but individual results ranged from seven years younger to nine years older than the person actually was.2 Treat it as one input with real noise in it, not a verdict, and be skeptical of anyone using it to sell you a package.

How often is it repeated?

Usually at around six months, and after any course of treatment aimed at pigment, texture or redness. The point of a baseline is the comparison that follows it. Absolute scores are the better thing to track: repeated captures in one study showed roughly 3 percent variation in absolute wrinkle scores against roughly 9 percent in percentiles, so a percentile that moves a few points between visits may be telling you nothing.2

Do I get to see the images?

Yes, in the room, on a screen, during the consultation. That is most of the reason for doing it first. It turns the appointment from a list of recommendations into a conversation about something you are both looking at.
