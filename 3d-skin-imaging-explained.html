What 3D skin imaging shows that a mirror cannot

The most useful photograph is not the one showing what you already know is there. It is the one showing what has not surfaced yet.

By Dr. Shankar Mundluru, MD MBA
Board-certified physician · Founder, Aahana Medical Aesthetics
September 5, 2026 · Updated November 24, 2026 · San Francisco

The short version

  • 3D imaging builds a measurable model of your face and neck, not just a picture of it.
  • It records shape as well as color, so volume, proportion and lift can be measured between visits.
  • Pigment and redness are seen separately, which matters most in deeper skin where they look alike.
  • Its real value is the baseline: the next visit is compared against a measurement, not a memory.
  • It is a measurement tool, not a diagnosis, and skin scores across skin types are not well studied.

A mirror gives you one view, in whatever light you happen to be standing in, filtered through a lifetime of opinions about your own face. It is a genuinely poor instrument, and it is the one almost every skin decision gets made with.

Standardized imaging removes the variables. Same position, same light, same cameras, every time. That alone makes two visits comparable, which a mirror and a memory never are.

What it actually measures

The system we use, Aura, is made in Switzerland by Hexagon. Multiple cameras and lighting units capture the face and neck in a single moment and build a photo-realistic 3D model, a digital twin.1 Its software analyses wrinkles, pores, brown spots, red areas and texture, and also expressions, angles, distances, volume and lift.12

The skin features are familiar. The structural ones are what a flat photograph cannot give you.

Shape is most of the story

Much of what changes with age is shape rather than color: volume lost from the temples and cheeks, a jawline that softens, a midface that descends. Much of what injectables and devices are meant to change is shape too. A photograph records none of it reliably. A 3D model measures it, so the difference between two visits is a number rather than an impression.

A photograph records color. A 3D model also records shape.

Separating red from brown

Pigment and redness are analysed separately.1 That is more useful than it sounds, and disproportionately useful in richly pigmented skin.

Discoloration across the cheeks could be melasma, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, background vascular flushing, or some combination. Against a deeper skin tone these can look nearly identical in the room. They are treated differently, and treating one as though it were another is how people get worse rather than better. Seeing them apart narrows the question before anyone touches the skin.

The limits, which nobody puts on their website

It measures, it does not diagnose. It does not identify skin cancer, and it is not a substitute for examination by a clinician. Anything concerning on your skin should be looked at whether or not it showed up on a scan.

Scores carry noise. A number that moves a little between visits may be telling you nothing. The images, and measured change in shape, are more trustworthy than any single score.

Skin types are under-studied. We have not found published data on how its skin scores perform across different skin types. That gap is familiar across this field, and it is a reason to read the images carefully rather than lean on a number, particularly in deeper skin.

How we use it

Every patient here starts with imaging, before any treatment is discussed. It shows what is actually present rather than what is most visible. It establishes the baseline that makes the next visit meaningful. And it lets you see your own face the way a clinician sees it, which turns a list of recommendations into a conversation about evidence you are both looking at.

The images are not the plan. They are the part of the plan you can check.

Common questions

What does 3D skin imaging measure?

A 3D system such as Aura builds a photo-realistic model of the face and neck and analyses wrinkles, pores, brown spots, red areas and texture, along with expressions, angles, distances, volume and lift. The model can be compared visit to visit.

How is 3D imaging different from a photograph?

A photograph records color. A 3D model also records shape, so it can measure volume loss, proportion and lift, which is much of what ageing and treatment actually change.

Is it accurate?

For tracking change on the same person, standardized capture is far more reliable than a mirror or a phone photo. Individual scores still carry noise, and we have not found published data on how skin scores perform across skin types, so the images and measurements matter more than any single number.

Does it work on darker skin?

Measurements of shape, volume and proportion do not depend on skin tone. Skin scores deserve more caution in deeper skin, where the separate views of pigment and redness are more useful than the numbers attached to them.

Can it detect skin cancer?

No. It measures and models; it does not diagnose. Anything concerning on your skin should be examined by a clinician whether or not it shows up on a scan.

Why take images before treatment?

Because the next visit is only meaningful against a baseline. Without one, judging whether a treatment worked means comparing today against a memory.
