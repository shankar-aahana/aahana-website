Understanding the Fitzpatrick scale, and why it's only part of the picture

It is a sunburn scale, not a pigment scale. That one distinction explains most of what goes wrong when it gets used as one.

By Dr. Shankar Mundluru, MD MBA
Board-certified physician · Founder, Aahana Medical Aesthetics
September 4, 2026 · San Francisco

The short version

  • The Fitzpatrick scale measures how skin reacts to sun, not what color it is.
  • It was built in 1975 to dose ultraviolet light in psoriasis treatment, with four categories, all describing lighter skin.
  • Objective color measurement and Fitzpatrick type correlate poorly. They are not interchangeable.
  • Types V and VI are the weakest part of the scale, holding the widest range of human pigmentation in the fewest boxes.
  • It also says nothing about how skin behaves: dryness, reactivity, how it heals, what it does when provoked.
  • It is a reasonable starting point and a poor stopping point.

If you have ever had a consultation for laser, a chemical peel or anything involving light, someone has assigned you a Roman numeral. Fitzpatrick II. Fitzpatrick IV. It goes in the chart, it shapes what gets recommended, and it is rarely explained.

It is worth understanding, because it is genuinely useful, and because knowing what it was designed to do tells you exactly where it stops working.

What the Fitzpatrick scale actually measures

Thomas Fitzpatrick, then chair of dermatology at Harvard, published the scale in 1975 to solve one clinical problem.1 Treating psoriasis with PUVA (psoralen, then ultraviolet A), he needed to predict how much UVA a patient could absorb before burning. Hair and eye color predicted poorly, so he asked a different question: what does your skin do in the sun?

Type IAlways burns, never tans
Type IIAlways burns, tans minimally
Type IIIBurns minimally, tans gradually
Type IVRarely burns, tans easily
V and VIAdded later

Every category describes reactivity. None describes color. That is not a flaw in the instrument; it is what the instrument was for.

It answers "how does your skin behave in sunlight," and gets asked "how much melanin do you have."

A second problem

The scale is largely self-reported, and self-report is least accurate in exactly the skin types where a mistake costs most.

Why V and VI are the weakest part

Types I to IVFine gradations

The original four describe lighter skin in detail.

Types V and VITwo boxes

Appended afterward, and asked to hold an enormous share of the world's pigmentation.

Light brown and deeply pigmented skin can both land in V depending on who assigns it, and they do not behave the same under a laser, in the sun, or after inflammation. A plan resting on that number alone fails there.

Tone is one axis. Tendency is another.

The Fitzpatrick number has a second gap, and in the treatment room it matters more than color. Two people can share a type and have skin that behaves nothing alike: one dry, fine and reactive; one oily, thick and tolerant; one that stays inflamed for days after a procedure the other shrugs off.

Prakriti, from Ayurvedic constitutional medicine, describes baseline tendency rather than surface: barrier fragility, a tilt toward heat and inflammation, or toward oil and congestion. It shapes how hard to treat and how fast to escalate, whether to repair a barrier for a month first, what healing looks like, and what holds a result between visits.

What they areDescriptions

A frame for observations attentive clinicians already make, useful for prompting better questions.

What they are notDiagnoses

Nobody is purely one type. The blend shifts with age, season, stress and hormones, and the same warning applies to the Fitzpatrick number.

So we use both, and hold both loosely. Neither one is the patient. The answers still come from her history and what her skin has actually done.

What to use alongside it

The scale should not be thrown out: sun reactivity is real information. It just should not be the whole assessment. At Aahana it is where the conversation starts.

Common questions

What is the Fitzpatrick scale?

The Fitzpatrick scale is a six-category system that classifies skin by how it reacts to sun exposure, ranging from type I, which always burns and never tans, to type VI, which rarely burns. It describes reactivity rather than skin color, and it is usually assigned by self-report or clinician judgment rather than measurement.

What was the Fitzpatrick scale originally used for?

Thomas Fitzpatrick developed it at Harvard in 1975 to determine safe ultraviolet A dosing for PUVA photochemotherapy in psoriasis patients. Hair and eye color had proven unreliable for predicting who would burn. The original system had four categories, all describing lighter skin; types V and VI were added later.

Is the Fitzpatrick scale accurate for darker skin?

It has real limitations there. Types V and VI were appended to a scale originally built around lighter skin, and between them they cover an enormous range of human pigmentation in only two categories. Self-reported classification is also least reliable in darker skin types, which is where misclassification carries the greatest risk.

Does the Fitzpatrick scale measure skin color?

No. It measures sun reactivity. When objective spectrophotometer measurements of skin color were compared with self-reported Fitzpatrick types in 115 subjects, the correlation was poor, with people reporting the same Fitzpatrick type spanning several objective color categories. Researchers concluded the two are separate methods that cannot be used interchangeably.

What does Ayurvedic constitution have to do with skin typing?

The Fitzpatrick scale describes how skin reacts to sun. It says nothing about how skin behaves in other ways: how dry it runs, how readily it inflames, how it heals. Ayurvedic constitutional assessment, or Prakriti, addresses that second axis, describing baseline tendencies such as dryness and barrier fragility, a disposition toward heat and inflammation, or toward oil and congestion. Used well it informs the whole approach: how aggressively to treat, whether to repair the barrier before introducing actives, what healing is likely to look like, and what maintenance a person needs between visits. But these are descriptions rather than diagnoses, and they are a frame for asking better questions rather than a set of boxes. Most people are a blend, and that blend shifts with age, season and stress. The same caution applies to the Fitzpatrick number: any typing system stops being useful the moment it replaces looking at the individual.

Does my Fitzpatrick type determine which laser is safe for me?

It informs the decision but should not decide it. Wavelength, fluence, pulse duration and cooling matter more, and a careful provider will also consider objective skin measurement and your history of how your skin has responded to injury or prior treatment. A history of dark marks after minor inflammation is often more predictive than the numeral in your chart.
