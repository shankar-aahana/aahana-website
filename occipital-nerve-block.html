Occipital nerve block

An injection near the nerves at the back of the head. Relief can begin within minutes, and how long it lasts tells us something.

Performed by Dr. Shankar Mundluru, MD MBA
Board-certified physician · Founder, Aahana Medical Aesthetics
San Francisco · Dogpatch
Reviewed November 2026

The occipital nerves run from the base of the skull up over the back of the head. When pain starts there and travels upward, those nerves are often where it is coming from. A block places an injection near them.

It acts quickly. Relief can begin within minutes, and it can last weeks. How long it lasts is useful in itself: it is what the decision to repeat is based on.

01

What it treats

Occipital neuralgia. Sharp, shooting or burning pain that starts at the base of the skull and travels up over the back of the head, often on one side. The scalp can be tender to touch between attacks.

Some migraines and cluster headaches. Not all of them. Where the pain pattern points to the occipital nerve, a block can help, and the response is part of how that is established.

Which category you are in is worked out at the consultation from your headache history, where the pain sits, and an examination of the area. It is not decided from a description over the phone.

02

What a session is like

You sit upright and lean forward slightly. The area at the base of the skull is cleaned, the landmarks are found by feel, and the injection is placed. It takes a minute or two.

Session length
About 15 minutes, and about 30 for a first visit with the assessment.
Sensation
Pressure and a brief sting. The area is often already tender.
Onset
Relief can begin within minutes.
How long it lasts
Days to several weeks. It varies a great deal between people.
Cadence
Repeated based on how long relief lasts, rather than on a fixed interval.
Downtime
None. Numbness over the area for a few hours is expected. Soreness or a small bruise for a day or two.

Common questions

How quickly does it work?

Relief can begin within minutes. The area at the back of the head usually goes numb for a few hours, and the headache can briefly return as that numbness wears off. Where a longer-acting component is included, its effect builds over a few days, so the first day is not the whole picture.

How long does it last?

It varies, from days to several weeks. The duration is not incidental information. It is used to decide when, or whether, to repeat the block, so noting the date it wore off is one of the more useful things you can do afterwards.

How often can it be repeated?

Based on how long relief lasted rather than on a fixed calendar. If a block holds for six weeks the interval follows that. If it holds for a few days, that is worth knowing too, and it usually changes the plan rather than simply repeating the same thing sooner.

Does it hurt?

There is pressure and a brief sting, and the area is often already tender, which people notice more than the needle. It takes a minute or two. Soreness or a small bruise for a day or two afterwards is common, and a cold pack for ten minutes at a time helps.

What is in the injection?

What goes into the block is decided at the visit, from your history and what the block is for. It is set out in the care instructions and gone through with you before anything is done, including the points that matter if you have diabetes.

Can I drive home?

Most people drive themselves home. For a first block it is worth having someone you can call, because a small number of people feel lightheaded afterwards. Take it easy for the rest of the day. Showering is fine.

Before your visit

There is no special preparation. A few things help us plan the treatment.

Bring

  • Your headache diary, or a rough count: headache days per month over the last three months, and how many of those were severe.
  • A list of your current medications, including what you take when a headache starts and anything you take to prevent them.
  • What you have already tried, and how well each thing worked.

Keep taking

  • Your usual headache and migraine medications, unless we have told you otherwise.
  • Prescribed blood thinners. Do not stop them. Tell us and we plan around them.

Tell us before

  • If you are pregnant or breastfeeding.
  • Any allergy to a numbing medication or to steroids.
  • If you have diabetes. A steroid in a nerve block can raise blood sugar for a day or two.
  • Any rash, cut or infection where the injection would go.
  • Previous blocks for headache: when, and how long relief lasted.

The day itself

  • Eat beforehand.
  • Clean hair and skin, with no hair products at the back of the head.
  • Most people drive themselves home. For a first block, have someone you can call if you feel lightheaded.

The first day

  • Numbness over the back of the head for a few hours is expected.
  • Relief can start within minutes. If a steroid was included, its longer effect builds over a few days, so a headache can briefly return when the numbness wears off.
  • Soreness or a small bruise for a day or two. A cold pack, ten minutes at a time, helps.
  • Take it easy for the rest of the day. Showering is fine.
  • If you have diabetes and a steroid was used, check your blood sugar more often for a day or two.

After that

  • Keep a headache diary, noting how long the relief lasts. That is what decides when, or whether, to repeat the block.
  • Keep taking your usual headache medications. This treatment does not replace them.

When to contact us straight away

  • Difficulty swallowing, speaking or breathing. Seek emergency care first, then call us.
  • A sudden severe headache unlike your usual one, or headache with fever and a stiff neck, confusion, or weakness or numbness beyond the treated area. Seek emergency care.
  • Any vision change, or a drooping eyelid.
  • Signs of infection at the injection site: spreading redness, warmth, swelling, pus or fever.
  • Dizziness or faintness that does not pass after resting.
