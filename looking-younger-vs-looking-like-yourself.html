The difference between looking younger and looking like yourself

These sound like the same request. They are not, and the distance between them explains most of what has gone wrong in this field.

By Dr. Shankar Mundluru, MD MBA
Board-certified in Internal Medicine · Founder, Aahana Medical Aesthetics
September 5, 2026 · San Francisco

Nobody walks into a consultation asking to look like someone else.

And yet you can stand on a street in this city and pick out the faces that have been worked on, not because any single thing is wrong with them, but because they have started to resemble each other. Same cheek. Same brow. Same flat, unreadable calm across the midface. Whatever those people asked for, it was not this.

So it is worth being precise about what is actually being requested, because two very different goals get expressed in the same sentence.

What people actually say

Almost nobody says make me look thirty. What they say is: I look tired. I look angry when I am not. I do not look like myself.

That last one is the most common and the most revealing. It is not a complaint about age. It is a complaint about recognition. Something has drifted between the face she has and the face she carries around in her head, and she wants the gap closed.

That is a completely different brief from make me younger, and the treatments that satisfy it are different too.

Youth is not smoothness

Here is where the industry's shorthand does real damage.

A young face is not a smooth face. Look at any twenty-five-year-old properly. There are hollows under the eyes and at the temples. There is asymmetry, always. There are lines that appear when she smiles and vanish when she stops. What a young face has is not the absence of features. It is projection, support, and a lot of movement.

Chase smoothness and you can remove every one of those things and arrive somewhere strange: a face with no shadows, no asymmetry and no expression, which reads not as younger but as still. Stillness is the tell. It is why overtreated faces are legible from across a room even when no single feature is obviously altered.

Youth is not the absence of texture. It is the presence of structure and movement.

Why treated faces converge

The convergence is not an accident of taste. It follows from method.

If treatment is organized around erasing features, everyone gets the same features erased, and the endpoint is the same for everybody. Fill every hollow, soften every line, lift every brow, and you are not restoring anyone. You are applying a template, and a template produces one face.

If treatment is instead organized around what changed in this person, the endpoint is as varied as the people. Facial fat sits in discrete compartments that deflate at different rates, and which of yours deflated first is specific to you. Restoring what receded gives you back your own face. Filling every shadow gives you somebody else's.

That is the whole distinction, and it is a technical one before it is an aesthetic one.

Individuality is the first thing lost

The features people are quickest to treat are frequently the ones that make a face specific: a strong nasolabial fold, a slightly heavy brow, a jaw that is not symmetrical, the line between the eyebrows that appears when someone concentrates.

Some of those genuinely bother people, and treating them is reasonable. But they are also, collectively, what makes a face recognizably one person rather than a category. Erase enough of them and you have removed the evidence that anyone in particular lives there.

I am not arguing against treatment. I do this for a living and I think it can be excellent. I am arguing that the question is which changes to reverse, not how many.

Restraint is the skill

The hardest thing in this field is not technique. Technique is learnable and most practitioners get there.

The hard part is knowing when to stop, and being willing to tell someone that the thing she is asking for will not give her what she actually wants. That conversation costs money to have. It is much easier to agree, do the treatment, and let her work out later that she looks smoother and less like herself.

A practice that never declines anything is not being generous. It has simply decided that its judgment is not part of what you are paying for.

The test

There is a reliable measure of whether any of this went well, and it is not a photograph.

It is what people say. If they tell you that you look rested, or well, or ask whether you have been on holiday, the work succeeded. If they ask what you had done, it did not, regardless of how good the individual result looks in a mirror.

Aging is not a defect to be corrected. It is a set of changes, some of which are worth addressing because they misrepresent you, and some of which are simply what a face looks like as it accumulates a life. Telling those apart is most of the job.

The goal is not a younger face. It is your face, well supported, still recognizably yours, for as long as possible.

Common questions

Why do people who have had cosmetic treatment often look alike?

Because treatment organized around erasing features produces the same endpoint for everyone. Filling every hollow, softening every line and lifting every brow applies a template rather than restoring an individual. Treatment organized around what specifically changed in one person produces results as varied as the people, since facial fat compartments deflate at different rates and in different orders from face to face.

What does looking natural after aesthetic treatment actually mean?

It means the structure and proportion that were lost have been restored rather than new features added. A young face is not smooth or symmetrical; it has hollows, asymmetry and expression lines that come and go. Removing all texture, shadow and movement does not read as younger, it reads as still, which is why overtreated faces are recognizable at a distance.

How do I know if a provider will overtreat me?

Ask what they would decline to do, and why. A provider who has never talked a patient out of something is not exercising judgment. Ask them to describe what specifically changed in your face rather than listing treatments, and notice whether the plan is built from that assessment or from a standard menu.

Should I treat every line and hollow?

Not necessarily. Some features genuinely misrepresent you, such as looking tired or angry when you are not, and treating those is reasonable. Others are simply what makes your face specific rather than generic. The useful question is which changes misrepresent you, not how many changes can be made.
