The difference between looking younger and looking like yourself

These sound like the same request. They are not, and the distance between them explains most of what has gone wrong in this field.

By Dr. Shankar Mundluru, MD MBA
Board-certified physician · Founder, Aahana Medical Aesthetics
September 5, 2026 · San Francisco

Nobody walks into a consultation asking to look like someone else.

And yet you can stand on a street in this city and pick out the faces that have been worked on, not because any single thing is wrong with them, but because they have started to resemble each other. Same cheek. Same brow. Same flat, unreadable calm across the midface. Whatever those people asked for, it was not this.

So it is worth being precise about what is actually being requested, because two very different goals get expressed in the same sentence.

What people actually say

Almost nobody says make me look thirty. What they say:

"I look tired.""I look angry when I am not.""I do not look like myself."

The last is the most common and the most revealing. It is not a complaint about age but about recognition: the face she has has drifted from the one she carries in her head, and she wants the gap closed. A different brief from make me younger, with different treatments.

Youth is not smoothness

Where the industry's shorthand does real damage. Look properly at any twenty-five-year-old:

A young face hasStructure and movement

Hollows under the eyes and at the temples. Asymmetry, always. Lines that appear with a smile and vanish after. Projection, support, and a lot of movement.

Chase smoothness and you getStillness

No shadows, no asymmetry, no expression. It reads not as younger but as still, which is why overtreated faces are legible across a room with no single feature obviously altered.

Youth is not the absence of texture. It is the presence of structure and movement.

Why treated faces converge

The convergence is not an accident of taste. It follows from method.

Erase featuresOne face

Fill every hollow, soften every line, lift every brow: the same endpoint for everybody. A template, not a restoration.

Restore what changed in youYour face

Facial fat sits in compartments that deflate at different rates, and which of yours went first is specific to you.

That is the whole distinction, and it is technical before it is aesthetic.

Individuality is the first thing lost

The features treated quickest are often the ones that make a face specific:

A strong nasolabial foldA slightly heavy browAn asymmetrical jawThe line between the brows when concentrating

Some genuinely bother people, and treating them is reasonable. Collectively, though, they make a face one person rather than a category. Erase enough and you remove the evidence that anyone in particular lives there.

I am not arguing against treatment. The question is which changes to reverse, not how many.

Restraint is the skill

LearnableTechnique

Most practitioners get there.

The hard partKnowing when to stop

And telling someone that what she asks for will not give her what she wants. That conversation costs money; agreeing is easier, and she learns later she looks smoother and less like herself.

Never declining is not generosity

A practice that never declines anything has decided its judgment is not part of what you are paying for.

The test

A reliable measure of whether this went well, and it is not a photograph. It is what people say.

It worked"You look rested."

Or well, or "have you been on holiday?"

It did not"What did you have done?"

However good the individual result looks in a mirror.

Aging is not a defect to correct. Some changes are worth addressing because they misrepresent you; some are what a face looks like as it accumulates a life. Telling those apart is most of the job.

Not a younger face. Your face, well supported, still recognizably yours, for as long as possible.

Common questions

Why do people who have had cosmetic treatment often look alike?

Because treatment organized around erasing features produces the same endpoint for everyone. Filling every hollow, softening every line and lifting every brow applies a template rather than restoring an individual. Treatment organized around what specifically changed in one person produces results as varied as the people, since facial fat compartments deflate at different rates and in different orders from face to face.

What does looking natural after aesthetic treatment actually mean?

It means the structure and proportion that were lost have been restored rather than new features added. A young face is not smooth or symmetrical; it has hollows, asymmetry and expression lines that come and go. Removing all texture, shadow and movement does not read as younger, it reads as still, which is why overtreated faces are recognizable at a distance.

How do I know if a provider will overtreat me?

Ask what they would decline to do, and why. A provider who has never talked a patient out of something is not exercising judgment. Ask them to describe what specifically changed in your face rather than listing treatments, and notice whether the plan is built from that assessment or from a standard menu.

Should I treat every line and hollow?

Not necessarily. Some features genuinely misrepresent you, such as looking tired or angry when you are not, and treating those is reasonable. Others are simply what makes your face specific rather than generic. The useful question is which changes misrepresent you, not how many changes can be made.
