Botox for chronic migraine

Preventive treatment on a twelve-week cycle. It works on the month rather than on today's headache.

Performed by Dr. Shankar Mundluru, MD MBA
Board-certified physician · Founder, Aahana Medical Aesthetics
San Francisco · Dogpatch
Reviewed November 2026

This is preventive treatment for chronic migraine. A series of small injections is placed across areas of the head, neck and shoulders, and the aim is to reduce how many headache days you have in a month. It is given every twelve weeks, and it is judged over months rather than over days.

It does not act on a headache you already have. Whatever you normally take when one starts, you keep taking. The two sit alongside each other.

01

What it treats

Chronic migraine: headaches on many days of the month, over months, with migraine features on at least some of them. It is for people whose month is shaped around headaches rather than interrupted by one occasionally.

Frequency is the thing that decides it, which is why the first question at consultation is how many headache days you have had per month over the last three months, and how many of those were severe. A headache diary answers that precisely. A rough count is enough to start the conversation.

What a session is like

You sit upright. The injections are placed across the forehead, the temples, the back of the head, the neck and the upper shoulders, in the same pattern each time. The needle is very fine and each injection is brief.

Session length
About 15 minutes, and about 30 for a first visit with the assessment.
Sensation
A series of quick stings. The neck and shoulders are the more tender part.
Numbing
Not usually needed.
Onset
Prevention builds over 2 to 4 weeks. Some people need two treatment cycles for the full benefit.
How long it lasts
Through the 12-week cycle, often fading toward the end of it.
Cadence
Every 12 weeks, given before the benefit fades rather than after.
Downtime
None. Stay upright and leave the sites alone for 4 hours. Mild headache or neck and shoulder soreness for 1 to 2 days is common.

Common questions

Will it help the headache I have today?

No, and it is not meant to. This is preventive treatment. It acts on how many headache days you have in a month rather than on any single headache, and it builds over two to four weeks. Keep taking whatever you normally take when a headache starts. Nothing here replaces that.

How soon will I know whether it is working?

Prevention builds over two to four weeks after the first session. Some people need two treatment cycles, so about six months, before the full benefit is clear. That is why the diary matters. Judging it at week two, or from memory rather than a record, is judging it too early and with the wrong instrument.

Where do the injections go?

Across areas of the head, neck and shoulders, in a standard pattern: the forehead, the temples, the back of the head, the neck and the upper shoulders. The pattern is the same from session to session, which is what makes it possible to compare one cycle with the next and adjust.

Does it change how my face looks?

The forehead and temples are part of the treatment area, so some softening of lines there is a common side effect of the treatment rather than its purpose. If you want that effect, or specifically do not want it, say so at the consultation. It changes how the forehead is approached.

Why every twelve weeks?

Because that is the interval the treatment is given on. Benefit tends to fade toward the end of the cycle, and the next session is given before it fades rather than after. Keeping to the twelve weeks is a large part of whether the treatment holds.

What does the session feel like?

The needle is very fine and each injection is brief. Most people describe a series of quick stings, with the neck and shoulders the more tender part. The whole thing takes about fifteen minutes and you walk out and carry on with the day, apart from staying upright and leaving the sites alone for four hours.

What is a headache diary and how detailed does it need to be?

A date, whether you had a headache, how bad it was, and what you took. That is enough. A phone note or a paper calendar both work. The point is a record made at the time rather than a recollection at the next visit, because recollection consistently underestimates good months and overestimates bad ones.

Before your visit

There is no special preparation. A few things help us plan the treatment.

Bring

  • Your headache diary, or a rough count: headache days per month over the last three months, and how many of those were severe.
  • A list of your current medications, including what you take when a headache starts and anything you take to prevent them.
  • What you have already tried, and how well each thing worked.

Keep taking

  • Your usual headache and migraine medications, unless we have told you otherwise.
  • Prescribed blood thinners. Do not stop them. Tell us and we plan around them.

Tell us before

  • If you are pregnant or breastfeeding.
  • Any rash, cut or infection where the injections would go.
  • Previous treatment of this kind: when, and how long the benefit lasted.

The day itself

  • Eat beforehand.
  • Clean hair and skin, with no hair products at the back of the head.
  • Most people drive themselves home.

The first four hours

  • Stay upright. Do not lie down for four hours.
  • Do not rub or massage the injection sites. Forehead, head, neck and shoulders.
  • No hard exercise, sauna or hot tub for the rest of the day.

The first weeks

  • Mild headache, or neck and shoulder soreness, for one to two days is common.
  • Prevention builds over two to four weeks. Some people need two treatment cycles for the full benefit.
  • Keep taking your usual headache medications. This treatment does not replace them.
  • Repeats every twelve weeks.

The headache diary

It is how we judge the result and adjust the next session, so it is worth doing simply enough that you keep doing it.

  • The date.
  • Whether you had a headache that day, and how bad it was.
  • What you took, if anything.

A phone note or a paper calendar both work. What matters is that it is written at the time rather than recalled at the next visit.

When to contact us straight away

  • Difficulty swallowing, speaking or breathing. Seek emergency care first, then call us.
  • A sudden severe headache unlike your usual one, or headache with fever and a stiff neck, confusion, or weakness or numbness beyond the treated area. Seek emergency care.
  • Any vision change, or a drooping eyelid.
  • Unusual neck weakness, or trouble holding your head up.
  • Signs of infection at an injection site: spreading redness, warmth, swelling, pus or fever.
  • Dizziness or faintness that does not pass after resting.
