Menopause and skin: what actually changes, and what helps

Patients describe their skin changing suddenly rather than gradually. They are describing something real, and it tracks estrogen loss rather than birthdays.

By Dr. Shankar Mundluru, MD MBA
Board-certified physician · Founder, Aahana Medical Aesthetics
September 5, 2026 · San Francisco

The short version

  • Roughly 30 percent of skin collagen is lost in the first five years after menopause.
  • That loss tracks the duration of estrogen deficiency more closely than it tracks chronological age.
  • Estrogen affects collagen, hydration, barrier function and wound healing, so several things change at once.
  • Hormone therapy demonstrably improves skin, and skin alone is not a reason to take it.
  • Retinoids and photoprotection do real work here and are available to everyone.

There is a particular consultation I have learned to listen for. A woman in her late forties or fifties says her skin changed, and she does not mean gradually. She means she noticed it over a season. It got drier, it stopped bouncing back, foundation started sitting in places it never used to, and a face that had always looked a certain way suddenly did not.

She is usually slightly apologetic about it, as though she is describing something vague. She is not. She is describing one of the better documented changes in skin biology, and almost nobody has explained it to her.

What estrogen was doing

Estrogen is not a background player in skin. It does four jobs at once:

  • StructureStimulates collagen synthesis and supports dermal elasticity.1
  • HydrationDrives water retention, and significantly raises dermal glycosaminoglycans and hyaluronic acid.2
  • BarrierThat water-holding capacity is what supports barrier function.2
  • RepairEnhances fibroblast migration and collagen deposition, which is why wound healing changes too.2

So when estrogen falls, all four shift together, which is why it registers as a whole-face event rather than a specific complaint.

What the numbers look like

~30%Of cutaneous collagen lost in the first five years after menopause.2 Nothing like the gradual decline through adult life.
DurationThe loss tracks how long estrogen has been deficient more closely than chronological age.2

That reframes it. Two women the same age can have meaningfully different skin depending on when menopause came; an early menopause has been accumulating the deficit longer.

The clock that matters is not your age. It is how long the estrogen has been gone.

Postmenopausal women show delayed re-epithelialisation and reduced collagen deposition compared with premenopausal controls,2 which matters for how aggressively anyone should be treated.

What hormone therapy does for skin

This is where being an internist, not only an aesthetics practitioner, changes my answer. The skin effects are real.2

30%increase in dermal thickness after a year of oral estrogen therapy
6.49%increase in skin collagen after six months in another trial
Lesswrinkling and better elasticity with hormone replacement, where deficiency had worsened both

The same review says its role for skin conditions still needs more robust evaluation.2

What it isA whole-person decision

Made on symptoms, heart and bone health, personal and family history and individual risk, with the clinician managing your menopause.

What it is notA skin decision

The skin benefit is a bonus, not an indication, and should not be marketed as one. What I can do is make sure nobody tells you the changes are imaginary.

One caution

Topical estrogen has been associated with melasma.2 In deeper skin, already at higher risk of pigment problems, that belongs in the conversation before anything starts. Melasma is hard enough to treat without provoking it.

What works without hormones

Most of what helps is available to everyone, and unglamorous.

  • Topical retinoidsThe most useful topical here: collagen synthesis, elasticity and new vessels,2 and it treats perimenopausal acne too. Start slowly; drier, slower-healing skin is less forgiving.
  • Rigorous sun protectionUV is breaking down the collagen you are trying to keep, and daily sunscreen measurably slows photoaging.
  • Barrier repairGentle non-foaming cleansers, no stripping, moisturizers that occlude. Much of "looking worse" is barrier, not structure, and it improves fastest.
  • Prescriptions where indicatedRetinoids and azelaic acid for acne, antiandrogens such as spironolactone where appropriate, minoxidil and other options for thinning hair. Medical decisions, not product picks.
  • In-clinic collagen workRadiofrequency and biostimulators suit the usual complaint, laxity, better than resurfacing. Slower healing means gentler settings and longer gaps between sessions.

What to be skeptical of

A market with a lot of money in it and not much evidence. The clinical literature is blunt about the lack of high-quality data behind many products here:2

Oral supplementsPhytoestrogensLED therapiesProbiotics

Not necessarily useless; simply not established, and often sold at the moment women feel most urgent.

If it is marketed for menopausal skin at a premium: what would the same money do as a prescription retinoid and a good sunscreen?

Common questions

How much collagen do you lose after menopause?

Approximately 30 percent of cutaneous collagen is lost in the first five years after menopause. That loss correlates more closely with the duration of estrogen deficiency than with chronological age, which is why two women of the same age can have quite different skin depending on when their menopause occurred.

Why did my skin change so suddenly at menopause?

Because estrogen supports several skin functions at once. It stimulates collagen synthesis, increases dermal glycosaminoglycans and hyaluronic acid that hold water, supports barrier function, and promotes fibroblast migration and collagen deposition in healing. When it falls, structure, hydration, barrier and repair all shift together, which registers as a whole-face change rather than one specific complaint.

Does hormone therapy help skin?

The measurable effects are real. One trial showed a 30 percent increase in dermal thickness after a year of oral estrogen, and another a 6.49 percent increase in skin collagen after six months, with improvements in wrinkling and elasticity. But the role of hormone therapy specifically for dermatological indications requires more robust evaluation, and menopausal hormone therapy is a whole-person decision based on symptoms and individual risk. Skin benefit is a bonus, not an indication.

Can menopause cause acne?

Yes. Acne affects roughly 25 percent of perimenopausal women, as falling estrogen shifts the balance with relatively unopposed androgens. Topical retinoids and azelaic acid help, and antiandrogens such as spironolactone are used where appropriate. Adult-onset acne in this window is common and treatable.

What is the best skincare for menopausal skin?

A topical retinoid, which stimulates fibroblast-mediated collagen synthesis and improves elasticity while also addressing perimenopausal acne; daily broad-spectrum sunscreen, since ultraviolet exposure degrades collagen in parallel; and genuine barrier support with gentle cleansers and occlusive moisturizers. Much of what looks like structural aging in this window is barrier dysfunction, which improves faster than anything else.

Is topical estrogen safe for the face?

It requires caution, and one specific concern matters for people prone to pigmentation: topical estrogen has been associated with the development of melasma. For patients with deeper skin tones, who are already at elevated risk of pigmentary problems, that should be discussed before starting. Any use of topical hormones should be supervised by a clinician who knows your history.
